Domestication History: The domestic cat (Felis catus) was domesticated from the African wildcat (Felis lybica) roughly 9,000 to 10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent region of the Near East. Domestication is believed to have begun when wildcats were drawn to early agricultural settlements to hunt rodents attracted to stored grain, forming a mutually beneficial relationship with humans that gradually led to tameness. Ancient Egyptians held cats in especially high regard from around 2,000 BCE onward, associating them with the goddess Bastet and sometimes mummifying them; killing a cat in ancient Egypt could carry severe penalties.

Anatomy and Senses: Cats have a flexible spine and retractable claws (except in the cheetah, a related felid) that they use for climbing, gripping prey, and self-defense. Their whiskers, or vibrissae, are highly sensitive touch receptors rooted deep in the skin that help cats judge spaces and detect nearby objects in low light. Cats have excellent night vision, requiring roughly one-sixth the light level humans need to see, thanks to a reflective layer behind the retina called the tapetum lucidum. Their hearing range extends up to about 64,000 Hz, considerably higher than the human range of about 20,000 Hz, allowing them to detect high-pitched sounds made by small rodents.

Behavior and Communication: Cats communicate through vocalizations, body language, and scent marking. Meowing is a vocalization pattern that adult cats direct mainly at humans rather than at other cats, having apparently developed as a way to solicit attention and food from people. Purring commonly signals contentment but can also occur when a cat is in pain or distress, and some researchers believe the frequency of purring may promote healing in bones and tissue. Tail position and ear orientation are key indicators of mood: an upright tail generally signals friendliness and confidence, while flattened ears often indicate fear or aggression. Cats are generally solitary hunters in the wild but can form social bonds with humans and other cats, particularly in colonies with abundant food sources.

Diet and Nutrition: Cats are obligate carnivores, meaning their bodies require nutrients found only in animal tissue to survive and cannot function on a purely plant-based diet. Unlike most mammals, cats cannot synthesize sufficient taurine, an amino acid essential for heart function and vision, so it must come directly from their diet; a taurine deficiency can lead to blindness and heart disease. Cats also require preformed vitamin A and arachidonic acid from animal sources, since they lack the enzymes needed to produce these from plant precursors the way many other animals can.

Reproduction and Lifespan: A cat's gestation period lasts approximately 63 to 65 days, typically producing a litter of one to six kittens. Kittens are born blind and deaf, opening their eyes at around 7 to 10 days old. Domestic cats kept exclusively indoors commonly live between 12 and 18 years, with some reaching their early twenties, while cats that roam outdoors face significantly higher risks from traffic, predators, and disease, which generally shortens their average lifespan. Spaying or neutering not only prevents reproduction but is also associated with a reduced risk of certain cancers and a longer average lifespan.

Health and Veterinary Care: Common health issues in domestic cats include dental disease, obesity, and, particularly in older cats, chronic kidney disease. Routine veterinary checkups, core vaccinations (such as against feline panleukopenia, feline calicivirus, and feline herpesvirus), and parasite prevention are widely recommended to maintain a cat's health throughout its life. Obesity in cats increases the risk of diabetes mellitus and joint problems, and is one of the most common preventable health issues seen by veterinarians in developed countries.

Popular Breeds: Common recognized cat breeds include the Siamese, known for its short coat, blue eyes, and vocal, social personality; the Maine Coon, one of the largest domestic cat breeds, recognized for its long, shaggy coat and friendly temperament; the Persian, distinguished by its long fur and flat face; and the British Shorthair, known for its dense, plush coat and round face. Most domestic cats worldwide, however, are not purebred but are domestic shorthair or domestic longhair cats of mixed ancestry.

Cats and Humans: The domestic cat is one of the most popular pets in the world, with hundreds of millions kept as companions across households globally. Studies on human-animal interaction suggest that owning a cat can be associated with reduced stress and lower blood pressure in some owners, alongside companionship benefits. Cats retain many behaviors from their wild ancestors, including a strong prey drive expressed through play, a preference for perching in elevated locations to survey their surroundings, and territorial scent-marking behavior such as rubbing their face and body against objects and people.
